# UT cannot afford unrealized talent.

The case for Silicon Hills Project at Innovation Tower

10 September 2026

UT has already attracted the people who could build the next generation of important companies. The urgent question is how much of that ability becomes real while they are here.

Every student who never finds a cofounder, never gets pushed beyond a promising side project, or concludes that their ambition belongs somewhere else represents an opportunity UT may never recover. The loss starts before a company exists. It is easy to miss precisely because there is no failed company to count.

Silicon Hills Project is our response. We have begun bringing ambitious builders together at Summit House in West Campus. We propose a 12-month partnership at Innovation Tower: Silicon Hills develops the student community, Arena organizes sustained company participation, and UT connects both to its research and institutional resources.

The talent is already here. Give it a reason, a place and the people to build with.

## UT has the ingredients. Its peers show what more could look like.

UT reports 55,000 students and $1.37 billion in annual research expenditures. McCombs ranks second nationally for undergraduate entrepreneurship in the Princeton Review's 2026 list. Those are real strengths. They make the gap in founder outcomes more consequential. [1, 2]

### A separate view of billion-dollar startup outcomes

The Unicorn Founder Board counts 444 founders who attended Stanford, 200 who attended Berkeley and 71 who attended UT. It covers US unicorn founders and was updated in June 2026. Its educational scope differs from PitchBook's undergraduate ranking. [5]

Berkeley's undergraduate founder count is 2.3 times UT's. Stanford's is 1.9 times UT's. These comparisons do not establish why the gap exists, but they make a compelling case for testing a stronger way to develop the talent UT already attracts. There should be more.

## Talent can be lost long before it leaves Texas.

The earliest loss happens when ability is never stretched. A capable engineer stays within assignments they can already complete. Someone with a serious technical interest never meets the person who would turn it into a product. A student sees an ambitious startup career as something available elsewhere, because they have never worked beside someone living it here.

That is the problem Silicon Hills Project is built to address. We want students to encounter harder problems, stronger peers and people who expect them to follow through. We want their environment to make building a normal part of the week, with enough continuity that a promising conversation can become a working relationship.

For UT, the cost begins with teams that never form and promising work that never reaches a customer. It can compound when graduates build their companies, hiring networks and mentoring relationships elsewhere. Every graduating class closes part of the window to help those relationships take root here. The available evidence does not quantify these losses across the university; this is the risk our proposed partnership would address.

Stanford and Berkeley offer useful examples of how to intervene early. Stanford's Mayfield Fellows program accepts students without a product idea and combines a close cohort with startup experience and mentorship. Berkeley's StEP accepts individuals without ideas and helps them find complementary cofounders. Both make room for the person before the company. [6, 7]

UT already has valuable places to go once a student is ready to develop a business. Forty Acres Founders, for example, asks applicants to bring a specific business idea. Silicon Hills can help more students reach that point by creating a daily community around their curiosity and work. It can also help them find the UT program that fits what they need next. [8]

> Every academic year gives UT a limited window to turn unfamiliar people into collaborators. Waiting consumes that window.

## Silicon Hills has already begun assembling the missing community.

We started Silicon Hills Project because we wanted to reach UT builders before they had a company, a pitch deck or even a settled idea. Summit House is the first expression of that belief: a place in West Campus where proximity can become a habit. The supplied deck describes a six-month pilot built around sustained contact among exceptional student builders. [11, 12]

The Daily Texan's September 2 reporting documents the student-led launch, the leased house, round-the-clock access and early support from investors and alumni. Its correction makes clear that the house was leased. The commitment was to create an operating environment students could use immediately. [10]

It also includes finance, mathematics, neuroscience and other disciplines. The 47 registrants reporting graduation in 2028-2030 give this work a meaningful runway: relationships formed now can develop over several years. [13]

The deck's 11 member profiles include experience at OpenAI, Anthropic, NVIDIA, Palantir and Roblox. They support a concrete starting point: the community is already reaching people with serious technical exposure. [11]

This is early proof of demand and of our ability to convene people. Durable companies, repeat participation and long-term retention still need to be measured. Innovation Tower would give us a larger setting in which to test the next part of the model, with clear responsibility for what happens after people arrive.

## The proposed floor could connect students to the work they want to do.

Innovation Tower could let students observe decisions, ask better questions and develop relationships through repeated contact with working companies. That is the opportunity to build around.

| Proposal element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Space and term | Approximately 26,627 square feet at 1300 Red River; a 12-month pilot. |
| University contribution | $0 base rent, a landlord improvement allowance and building services. |
| Operating model | Arena operates and sublets the floor; tenant revenue supports operations and free student participation; UT's share is unspecified. |
| Student commitments | 40 seats, 25+ placements, 30+ sessions and two reports each year. |
| Governance | Arena runs tenants and programming; UT retains a proposed veto and an exit for missed student outcomes. |
| Current status | Private proposal. Tenant leases are unsigned. Access terms need agreement; no full operating budget is provided. [9] |

The concept includes a 120-seat Forum, daily lunch, a staffed front desk and The Forty's desks, lockers and interview room. It targets 10-20 companies. Arena reports 277 members at its existing institution; tower tenancies remain prospective. [9]

Discovery to Impact already occupies roughly 26,000 square feet on the sixteenth floor, and UT operates the Texas Innovation Center. A student community here could connect more builders to existing support. [14]

An engineer could help a student find a serious technical problem. A founder could explain a rejected prototype. An employer could watch someone's work develop before offering an internship. These are benefits to design for and measure.

The operating question is who brings the students back, helps newcomers enter the room and follows up when an introduction goes nowhere. Silicon Hills Project should take responsibility for that student experience. That is the missing piece we would bring to the proposal.

## Silicon Hills should own the student community.

Our proposed role is to connect the trust built in West Campus to the opportunities available at Innovation Tower. Silicon Hills is not named in the current proposal; this is the partnership we believe should be added. [9]

### Silicon Hills Project

Recruit across majors and class years. Welcome students before they have a business. Organize recurring work sessions, connect complementary builders and track whether participants keep building. Give every introduction an owner and a next step.

### Arena Hall

Develop the company and professional relationships that students can learn from. Translate those relationships into recurring office hours, useful project exposure, customer conversations and paid opportunities. Coordinate the floor's daily operation.

### UT and Discovery to Impact

Connect participants to research, faculty and existing programs. Set access expectations, designate an accountable university owner and review student outcomes. Help promising teams find the right institutional support without starting their search from scratch.

Summit House can remain the accessible community base in West Campus. Tower participation should extend that community through scheduled work blocks and clear opportunities. The partnership should test the practical details, including travel time, academic schedules and building access, before assuming students will use both spaces regularly.

A named student cohort also needs a wider route in. Open applications, peer referrals and project demonstrations can reach people who are not yet visible to faculty or investors. Selection should reward curiosity, work and generosity toward other builders. Existing networks should help discover students without becoming the only way to gain access.

Paid technical work can strengthen this model. A student can learn how a demanding company operates, develop financial breathing room and return with better problems to solve. The community should support students who want to found companies and those who want to build inside them.

## A credible next step needs credible economics and access.

The proposal asks UT to contribute something valuable. Using its annualized $42-per-square-foot asking-rent benchmark, also advertised by JLL, the requested floor represents roughly $1.12 million a year. That is a reference point for the contribution, not a forecast of rent UT would otherwise collect. Improvements and services add costs of their own. [9, 15]

A tenant-funded model needs a budget that works under realistic occupancy. The partners should show the cost of staffing, food, programming, fit-out and student support; the rent expected from signed tenants; and who covers a shortfall. University support should buy defined student benefits that remain credible if leasing takes longer than expected.

The physical plan also needs reconciliation. The concept's room allocations total 27,100 square feet, exceeding its stated floor plate by 473. Gauntlet AI tenancy, SpaceX teaching and Trailblazers participation remain proposed or unsigned. They should become supporting commitments as they are secured. [9]

Student access deserves the same attention as commercial occupancy. Agree building hours, event access, food service and use of shared facilities before recruiting around them. Summit House's 24/7 model cannot simply be assumed at the tower. A student's experience will depend on whether the promised workspace and people are available when their schedule allows them to participate.

Silicon Hills should be funded for a defined operating role, with student benefit as the basis for evaluation. The deck's later residency, placement-revenue and fund ideas are separate future possibilities. Neither those ideas nor projected sponsorship income establish how this tower partnership would pay its bills today. [11]

> Agree what students receive, who delivers it and how it is funded before committing to expansion.

## Scale the relationships, and prove what they produce.

The partnership should begin with a bounded operating plan. Space only helps when someone has the time, budget and responsibility to make it useful. We propose the following sequence; these are recommendations for negotiation, not commitments already secured.

### Before opening

Name one owner from UT, Arena and Silicon Hills. Agree a funded staffing plan, actual student access hours and a route in from West Campus. Confirm usable space, tenant commitments and the conditions under which student programming can begin.

### First 90 days

Recruit an initial cohort through both nominations and open discovery. Establish weekly work sessions and recurring operator contact. Record an entry baseline for every participant: current project, collaborators, experience and what they need next.

### Months 4-6

Review repeat use, work shipped, team formation and the quality of introductions. Ask students who stopped attending why they left. Adjust the schedule and support before expanding the cohort.

### Months 7-12

Evaluate paid placements, customer tests, sustained teams and movement into UT programs. Track participants' Austin and Texas plans. Renew or expand against documented results and a credible operating budget.

The tower's proposed annual targets provide an initial accountability framework. Silicon Hills should add measures of sustained building: 30- and 90-day return rates, prototypes tested by real users, teams still working together and introductions that lead to a concrete next step. Count verified paid placements separately from introductions and offers. [9]

Retention should be measured directly through follow-up with participants, including people who leave the program. A useful pilot can show whether the community helped a student build, work or remain in Texas. Demonstrating an effect on university-wide founder output will take longer and require a more rigorous comparison.

## UT should give this community a path to institutional scale.

The competitive risk is cumulative. When ambitious students find their collaborators elsewhere, future teams, hiring relationships and mentorship networks can develop elsewhere too. When they never find those collaborators at all, the loss is harder to see. Either way, UT has less opportunity to turn the ability it attracts into companies and careers rooted here.

Silicon Hills Project gives UT something concrete to build with: a student-led community, a working space and evidence that technically ambitious people want to participate. Arena brings a different set of relationships. The university brings research, programs and the ability to connect this effort to a much larger institution. Together, they can give a student community access to opportunities it cannot create alone.

The case for acting now rests on the students already here. They are choosing what to work on, who to work with and where to build their careers. Silicon Hills can help UT become more present in those decisions through useful daily experience. A measured pilot would let the university support that work while learning exactly where it helps.

We are asking UT and Arena to develop the student component with Silicon Hills Project, name responsible owners and negotiate a measured 12-month pilot. The first deliverable should be a jointly agreed plan for student access, staffing, costs and outcomes.

> UT has a community ready to help more students build here. The next step is to put the people, space and accountability behind it.
